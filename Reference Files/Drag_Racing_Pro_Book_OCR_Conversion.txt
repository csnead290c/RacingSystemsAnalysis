Chapter 2 ~ Motorsports Standard Atmosphere
Understanding the effects of changing weather conditions is a core issue for Motorsports
Engine dyno data is "corrected" back to a standard, reference set of ambient weather
conditions and every racer already knows that if the weather changes, the performance of
their racecar is also going to change
The description of “weather” for Motorsports must include three fundamental parameters
+ pressure, temperature and water vapor content. We all know that the higher the
barometric pressure, the faster our car will go... and also that the higher the
temperature, the slower our car will go.
Historically, these three fundamental parameters have been combined (in various ways)
into a measure of the ambient air’s density. More precisely, Motorsports is really
concemed with the ambient air's “oxygen” density, as a rising water vapor content will
displace the available oxygen molecules, and reduce the engine output. More on this
water vapor effect in Chapters 6 and 7.
Air's density is proportional to the ratio of absolute pressure and absolute temperature
The term absolute in this definition is very importam Absolute pressure refers to a total
vacuum as the base, that is, 0.0 in Hg (or 0.0 psia) Absolute temperature refers to
absolute zero as the base, roughly ~-460 deg Fahrenheit (degF). Absolute temperature is
usually measured in degrees Rankine (degR) These two systems of temperature
measurement are related by the following simple equation:
degR = degF + 459.67
Industry Standards
Now, back to the density of air. We normally refer to the air density relative to a
standard set of ambient weather conditions. But at least three different standards are
currently in use, depending on which industry you're working in!
© Aerospace = 59 degF, 14.696 psia (sea level), dry air (no water vapor)
© Automotive = 77 degF, 1000 mbar, 10 mbar water vapor pressure
* Motorsports = 60 degF, 29 92 in Hg (sea level), dry air (0% relative humidity)
note: | atm = 29.92 in Hg = 1013 25 mbar = 14.696 psia

You can see that the reference, standard conditions for Motorsports are different from
either the Aerospace or Automotive industry standards. The Motorsports standard is
closest to the Aerospace standard, differing by only 1 degF in ambient temperature at sea
level - the sea level pressure is the same. I don’t know who started using 60 degF rather
than $9 degF for the standard Motorsports temperature at sea level, but more on the effect
of this choice (and the complications that arise) in the next chapter
We all know that the “ambient® pressure decreases with increasing altitude, from about
(4.7 psia at sea level to only 3.3 psia at 35,000 ft. It also gets colder the higher in
elevation you go.
To simplify and standardize things, an international committee was convened in 1976,
and the definition of an aerospace “standard almosphere” was created It equals the
average pressure and temperature all over the planet for any given altitude (or elevation)
This definition, known as the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) has lead to a
great deal of confusion for many Motorsports people
And this gets to the core purpose of this book. That is, to document and publish the
technical information required to precisely define the Motorsports Standard
Atwosphere (MSA) so that everyone in the Motorsports industry is using the same
standard and methods. The Aerospace industry uses the ISA method and the Motorsports
industry the MSA method.
From here on out, we are going to use the MSA standard of 60 degF, 29 92 in Hg (sea
level) and dry air. At these standard conditions, let's call the “air densily index” = 100%
The actual density of air at these conditions can be calculated as 0.07633 Ibm/ft*3.
That's not a very big number! One pound of air at sea level takes up 13 1 cubic feet of
volume - that’s a cube 28.3 inches on each side.
For elevations (or altitudes) above sea level, and for temperatures warmer than 60 degF,
the “air density index” will always be less than 100% Simple enough at this point!
However, the TV weatherman and airline pilots have pulled another fast one on us when
it comes to the term "barometric pressure”. Recall that the “ambient” pressure decreases
with increasing altitude, from almost 30 in Hg at sea level to only 23 in Hg at 7,100 ft.
Whenever the “actual” ambient pressure equals the corresponding “standard atmosphere”
pressure for the “actual” elevation, the “corrected” barometric pressure is always 29.92 in
Hg

The Motorsports Standard Atmosphere (MSA) temperature at sea level is 60 degF. That
is 1 degF hotter than the Inmemational Standard Atmosphere (ISA) value of 59 degF. I
don't know who decided to make this change from ISA or the reasons for it
However, I have seen published data in a Hot Rod Magazine article by Steve Green
(August 1972) where 60 degF was being used as the sea level standard temperature. This
article states that the weather correction methods came from the Chrysler Corporation
Engineering group, probably in support of the Motown Missile and Mopar Missile
factory backed drag racing programs
Today, over 36 years later, virtually atl of the Motorsports weather stations and dyno data
reduction software use 60 degF as the standard reference temperature for sea level
Therefore, 60 degF must be the defacto Motorsports standard at this time.

Chapter ¢ - MSA Pressure Definition
At sea level the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) pressure is defined as 1013.25
mbar. In Motorsports we normally measure the barometric pressure in inch Hg and
1013.25 mbar is exactly the same as 29.92 in Hg. And 29.92 in Hg is clearly the
Motorsports Standard Atmosphere (MSA) pressure at sea level
Perfect Gas Law
T am going to use the Perfect Gas Law in the definition of both the ISA and MSA.
pressure variations. The Perfect Gas Law relates the air’s density to the pressure and
temperature based on Thermodynamics. Page 2-2 included the calculated air density at
the MSA sea level conditions 10 be 0.07633 lbmv*3 from the Perfect Gas Law.
RHO =P/(R*T)
where: RHO = air density
P = absolute pressure of air
R = gas constant for air
T = absolute temperature of air
Sample Calculation P = 1013.25 mbar = 29.92 in Hg = 14 696 psia
R = 53.3478 A-lbfTbmvdegR (for dry air)
T = 60 degF + 459.67 = 519.67 degR
RHO = (144 * 14 696) / (53.3478 * 519 67) = 0.07633 Ibavite3
where 144 = conversion constant from in”2 to R*2
ISA Pressure Definition
The method used by the ISA to define the pressure variation with increasing altitude is
based on simple laws or rules from both Fluid Mechanics and Thermodynamics. First,
the atmosphere is assumed to be in hydrostatic equilibrium. Imagine a very tall vertical
column of motionless air trapped by an invisible tube of pipe Since there is no
movement of the still air within the tube, the air in the lower portion must be at a greater
pressure in order to support the weight of all the air abave it (miles and miles of it!).

The Fluid Mechanics equation for hydrostatic equilibrium is used to define how the
variation in pressure within this tall column of air is related to the density of the air and
the effects of gravity.
dP /dALT - - GC * RHO
where. dP / dALT = derivative of pressure with respect to altitude
GC = gravitational constant
The ISA method further assumes that the atmosphere obeys the Perfect Gas Law So the
air density (RHO) in the equation above can be replaced from the Perfect Gas Law (page
4-1)
dP /dALT =-GC*P/(R°T)
Chapter 3 included the definitions of both the ISA and MSA temperature variations with
altitude (ALT). In their general format, the temperature variation can be stated as:
T=TO-TLR* ALT
where: TO = standard absobute temperature at AIT: 0 (sea level)
TLR — temperature lapse rate with altitude — 0.00356616 degR/ft

mathematical integration process results in
dP /P =(-GC/(R * (TO - TLR * ALT)) * dALT
Integrating both sides of this equation
In(P / PO) = (GC /(R * TLR)) * In(TO - TLR * ALT)/ TO)
where: Jn = natural logarithm
PO = standard absolute pressure at ALT ~ 0 (sea level)
‘The exponential form of this equation becomes
(P / PO) = (TO - TLR * ALT) / 70) * (GC /(R * TLR))

Frequent Motorsports weather questions involve the variety of methods used to describe
how much water is in the ambient air A number of descriptions are common:

© relative humidity

© vapor pressure

© dew point

A oumber of different devices can be used to measure and determine these values.

First things first - let me emphasize that all these methods are really just differen ways
of describing the same thing. It’s like measuring the weight of your racecar. You could
use grams, ounces, pounds, kilograms, or even tons - the numerical values ase all
different - but the actual weight (or mass) of the racecar is always the same. And so it
goes with water vapor content.

Three Devices for Measuring Water Vapor Content

In Motorsports today, the most common device used to determine the ambient air's water
vapor conteat is the “Hair Hygrometer”. This is what’s inside all those weather station
boxes you see at the racetrack, and all the digital hygrometers for sale at Radio Shack.
Basically, the electrical resistance (or capacitance) of a special “hair” (or some other
“hygroscopic” material) is measured. The measured resistance is used with a calibration
curve contained in the electronics along with the measured ambient temperature to
calculate and display the relative humidity. Some of these devices also use the
electronics to calculate and display other quantities, typically the dew point. If the
weather station also measures barometric pressure, then the vapor pressure may also be
calculated and displayed
The second most commonly used device is the “Wet and Dry Bulb Psychrometer” —
either a hand-held sling psychrometer or a fan-aspirated psychcometer Basically,
ambient air is forced over two identical thermometers, one of which is covered with
absorbent materia! and wet with distilled waler. One thermometer measures the dry bulb
temperature and other the wet bulb temperature, which will always be lower that the dry
bulb temperature due to the cooling eftect from the evaporation of the distilled water.
The wet bulb temperature will always be somewhere between the dew point and dry bulb
temperatures. Psychrometric charts, equations, or PC software ase then used to determine

the vapor pressure and relative humidity from the dry and wet bulb temperatures
A third method, used pnmarily in laboratories and clean room manufacturing processes,
is called the “Optical Dew Point Hygrometer" and is the most accurate. It directly
determines the dew point temperature by chilling a given sample of air until dew or frost
forms on a small mirror. The presence of the dew is detected optically based on how the
mirror reflects light. This device can measure the dew point temperature to within
fractions of adegree Sophisticated electronics can then calculate any of the other water
vapor values as desired.
Describing Water Vapor Content
Relative Humidity - the definition of relative humidity was first presented in Chapter 5
Relative humidity (RH%) tells us how close to “saturated” the air is with water vapor.
Air can only hold so much water vapor before it condenses out as dew, frost or rain. At
100% relative humidity the air contains all the water vapor it can, The vapor pressure at
this “saturated” or 100% relative humidity condition can be determined from the 100%
line (saturation pressure) on the Relative Humidity Chast presented on page 7-3
Vapor Pressure - the definition of vapor pressure was also first presented in Chapter 6.
Out of the measured “total” barometric pressure, water vapor pressure is the “partial”
pressure exerted by the water vapor molecules Water vapor pressure can also be used to
determine how much water vapor is in the air on a volume basis More on this later,
Dew Point - the dew point is generally a calculated parameter to describe the amount of
water vapor in the air. The dew point temperature is the temperature whose saturation
pressure is the same as the ambient water vapor pressure If you were able to take a
sample of ambient air and coul it, the temperature at which dew of frost first occurred
would be the dew point temperature.
Graius - really a shortcut name for grains of water vapor per pound (GPP) of dry air A
grain is simply a measure of weight, like grams or pounds There are 7000 grains in a
pound, so | grain is a very small quantity GPP describes the actual weight of the water
vapor in a pound of dry air and is sometimes described as “specific humidity”, since it
uses a macs basis for the definition instead of a volume basis like vapor pressure.
‘Wet Bulb - the measured wet bulb temperature from a psychrometer The wet bulb
temperature is used with the dry bulb temperature to calculate the vapor pressure.

Sample HP Correction Factor Equation
Now it’s time to get a littke deeper into the math for the HP Correction Factor (HPC).
The overall format for the HPC comes from the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE)
The SAE Engine Power Test Code committee published the J1349 Standard Method in
August of 2004. You can go to the SAE website (www.SAE org) and order a copy of
this standard for yourself
For Motorsports weather correction, we have to tweak the SAE J1349 Standard a little.
‘We need to change from the SAE reference conditions and use the Motorsports Standard
Atmosphere (MSA) reference conditions of sea level, 60 degF and dry air instead We
also need to define two new terms, Theta and Delta, to make it easier to write out the
HPC equation into a cleas and concise format

Theta = (DEGF + 459 67) / (60 + 459.67) = (DEGF + 459.67) / 519.67

Delta = (PBAR - PWV) / 29.92
You might recognize the format of these equations from the earlier chapters, Theta is
simply the ravo of the ambient temperature (in degR) to the MSA temperature for sea
level (519.67 degR = 60 degF). Delta is the ratio of the ambient “dry” air pressure to the
MSA barometric pressure at sea level (29.92 in Hg) The “dry” air pressure is the total
barometric pressure (PBAR) less the partial water vapor pressure (PWV).
We need only one more variable for the SAE J1349 Standard method . . . the mechanical
efficiency of the engine. When an engine is cunning at wide-open throttle (WOT) on the
dyno, some of the power being developed inside each of the cylinders is lost to friction.
There are many sources of engine friction, including the rings and pistons sliding on the
cylinder walls, the rods and crank slinging oil all around the crankcase, bearing friction
everywhere —_. including the cam, lifters, pushrods and rocker arms, as well as many
other locations. Any belt driven accessories like oil pumps or superchargers also reduce
the engine power at the Nywheel
The engine's mechanical efficiency (ME%) puts a numeric value on these friction losses
For instance, a ME% of 80% means that 80 percent of the power available inside the
cylinders finds it’s way to the flywheel, either to be measured on the engine dyno or
delivered to the clutch or torque converter in the racecar. The actual ME% of an engine
varies with both RPM and thraitle position (ie load)

Other Weather Correction Factors
There are a few other factors that must also be considered in a comprehensive HPC
equation to get acceptable precision for Motorsports. These include:
© the effect of not changing the carb jetting to maintain a constant fuel-air ratio as the
weather changes. The engine may be getting richer or leaner as the weather changes.
* the variation in gas constants with higher water and fuel vapor content.
© the effects of very high water vapor content (greater than 0 40 in Hg or 60 grains).
Very high water vapor content is the largest of the three effects above. And this
correction is over and above the PWV effect included in the definition of Delta on page
8-4 The indicated thermal efficiency of the engine is reduced by the influence of water
vapor on the specific heats of the pre- and post-combustion gases and during the
combustion process itself Water vapor slows down the combustion and increases the
time loses unless the spark is properly advanced as the water vapor content increases.
There is very little empirical data to describe this very high water vapor content effect for
modern racing engines. The best data [ have seen is on page 431, Volume | of “The
Internal Combustion Engine in Theory and Practice” by Charles Fayette Taylor. It
includes a non-linear function, that predicts a 1.0% loss in HP for a PWV = 1.11 in Hg,
or about 168 GPP at sea level. This would correspond to a 1.0% increase in HPC. Note
that this data is for naturally aspirated, gasoline burning cngines only.
Generally the other effects are smaller and anyway the math is a whole lot more
complicated than that included in this book. And there are no “standard” methods for
these calculations anyway Better to leave all this to Motorsports engineers and software
progsams or maybe discussions at future SAE Motorsports conferences And this is
another reason why no two hand-held weather instruments ever get the same answer.

The Altitude Correction Factors (Factors) are first and foremost used to “convert” an ET
and MPH back to near sea level conditions to determine NHRA National Records. The
Factors are also used at tracks classified as altitude facilities, to determine the Comp
qualifying order, and the “handicap” or “head start” during eliminations. Each Comp
Class Index is adjusted for altitude by simply multiplying the Index by the Factor.
The Factors need to be as accurate as possible so that every Comp racer has an equally
fair chance to set a record or win a race, regardless of where they are racing.
Legacy NHRA Altitude Correction Factors
“The Factors were originally generated by Farmer Dismuke based on tables in aircraft
engine manuals that described power loss of engines at altitude. Since their original
introduction they have been modified at least twice. They never took into account the
competitors changing engine, driveline, and aerodynamic combinations to adapt to
various track/altitude conditions.”                       Jim Collins - NHRA, February 2010
NHRA created the Factors over 40 years ago, long before the PC .. . and even before
hand beld calculators. Bil! ‘Farmer’ Dismuke used either a slide rule or ran the numbers
on a big mainframe computer somewhere. Maybe he got help from Chrysler Engineering
at the time? The article, "Drag Strip Weather Corrections” by Steve Green from the
August 1972 issue of Hot Rod Magazine includes a description of the “classic” weather
correction methods for drag racing. And that would have been a great place to start!

The upper dashed line on the graph, labeled “sea level”, would be for an altitude

the effect of shifting the line labeled “sea level” to the right.

As you can see, above 80% nitromethane by volume the NSat ADI constant rises quickly.
The last two sample calculations (N100Sat and N90Sat) also showed that as you add
methanol to the fuel mixture (from 0% to 10%), the value of TSTAR drops You can
also see this behavior in the shape of the right hand portion of the line in this graph
These results clearly demonstrate that as you change the percentage of methanol in the
higher range of nitromethane fuel mixtures (above 80%), it has a real impact on the
sirflow rate of the engine The blower inlet air density is inversely related to the value of
the NSat ADI constant.
For example, increasing the nitromethane percentage from 80% to 90% not only adds
more nitromethane fuel to the engine (ie richer}... it also decreases the engine air flow
rate... making the engine even more rich. The NSat ADI weather correction method
properly accounts for all these complex interactions.
Finally, all the FueiSat ADI equations - MSat, GSat and every NSat . .. use the same
universal format. They all include a unique constant out front, the dry air pressure
(PAIR), and the cooled saturated temperature (TSTAR) of the blower inlet air after the
evaporative cooling process.
FuelSat ADI = Constant * PAIR / (TSTAR + 459.67)
where: PAIR and TSTAR are determined from the sohition of the energy

balance equations for whatever combination of ambrem weather

conditions that are present, and with the selected fuel type Constant.
